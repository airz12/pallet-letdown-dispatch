ON THE FLOOR

What it changed

The board runs every shift at a single distribution center. Two forklift operators work calls from it, and two warehouse managers send them. The desk puts the saving at roughly 40 minutes a shift — time that used to go on repeating requests over the radio, chasing down who took what, and walking back from a slot that turned out to be empty.

The harder number comes next. Every call now carries its own timestamps, so pickup time and door-to-drop cycle time come straight off the shift log rather than out of anyone's memory.

THE PROBLEM

What a radio call leaves behind

Replenishment in a rack-and-pick warehouse runs on shouted requests. The operations desk needs a pallet of something brought down from a reserve beam to the pick face, so it radios whoever is nearest. That works until it doesn't: two people think the other took it, a driver arrives at a slot that's already empty, or a trailer waits at a door while a request sits unheard.

None of it is written down. There's no record of what was asked for, who took it, how long it sat, or why a pallet never arrived — so the same failure repeats next week with nothing to point at.

Case pack48
Drop atDock 3

The forklift screen, drawn to read from a seated operator's distance. Slot address first, because that's the only thing needed to start moving.

The screen is modelled on the paper tag stapled to racking, because that's the document this replaces. The slot address is set at the largest size on the page — an operator glancing down from a mast needs the aisle, bay and level before anything else.

The last pair of digits carries the whole point of the job: 00 is the floor-level pick face, 01 through 03 are reserve beams overhead. A letdown is always a move from one of those upper levels to the floor.

Everything else on the tag answers a question that would otherwise come back as a second radio call: how many pallets, what the case pack is, and which door or lane it's going to.

THE FLOW

Three screens, one shared board

1

The desk calls the pallet

Search the full catalog by code or name, set how many pallets, where it's going and how urgent it is. The slot is filled in automatically and can be overridden. The ticket is numbered and sent.

2

The truck gets it and works it

The forklift screen chimes and shows the tag. Take this call → pallet is on the forks → dropped. Three taps, sized for gloves, each one timestamped. Anything blocking the job goes back as an exception instead of a shrug.

3

The shift log keeps the evidence

Every call lands in a log with its full trail, alongside shift metrics: calls waiting, average pickup time, door-to-drop cycle time, cases moved, and anything past its response target.

DECISIONS

Where the design does the work

Urgency is a clock, not an adjective

Three tiers carry response targets — routine 30 minutes, expedite 10, hot 5. The board counts up against the target and turns red when a call passes it, so "urgent" means a number a supervisor can hold someone to rather than a tone of voice.

Failure gets its own vocabulary

Six exception codes cover what actually stops a letdown: slot empty, pallet damaged, aisle blocked, wrong SKU in slot, load unstable, truck down. The call goes back to the desk with its reason attached, where it can be re-issued or closed out. Those codes are also the first real dataset on why replenishment fails here.

Priority sorts the queue, age breaks the tie

The forklift screen shows one call at a time — the highest priority, oldest first — so there's no judgment call about what to grab next, and a routine request can't sit behind newer traffic forever.

Case pack travels with the ticket

Quantities read as 2P + 3cs on the board and resolve to total cases underneath, because a desk thinks in pallets and a pick face runs out in cases. Case packs come straight from the product file, so the desk never has to work the arithmetic out.

Nothing depends on an install

It runs in a browser on whatever tablet or phone is already mounted to the truck. One file, no app store, no new hardware to approve.

Wiring it to the ERP

The board carries its own copy of the catalog and generates slot addresses. That is enough to run the workflow end to end, but a floor needs its addresses from the system of record. The next build puts that behind it, scoped against Fishbowl Inventory:

Read: parts, locations, on-hand quantity

Real slot addresses instead of generated ones, and a desk that can't call down a pallet from a slot the system already shows as empty.

Write: a location move on delivery

When the operator confirms the drop, the move posts back from the reserve slot to the pick face with the ticket number in the note, so inventory and the shift log agree without anyone keying it twice.

Credentials sit in a bridge service on the warehouse network rather than in the page, so the tablets never hold a login to the inventory system.
